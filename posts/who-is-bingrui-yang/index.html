Who is Bingrui Yang, the Apple engineer who decided RVs were broken?

Nine years leading iPhone development. Hardware chief at two robotaxi companies. Then one bad family camping trip in the summer of 2020 — and a decision to rebuild the travel trailer from scratch.

PF
PebbleFlowRV TeamAugust 2026 · 10 min read
Leaderboard ad · 970×90

Most RV companies are founded by RV people. Pebble was founded by someone who took one trip and came home irritated.

That detail explains more about the Pebble Flow than any spec on the sheet. It is not a trailer designed by someone who loves trailers and wanted to make a nicer one. It’s a trailer designed by a consumer-electronics engineer who looked at the category, decided the fundamental user experience was broken, and rebuilt it using the toolkit he had — which happened to be the iPhone and self-driving cars.

Nine years on the iPhone

Bingrui Yang holds a degree from Columbia University and spent roughly nine years at Apple, where he rose through engineering roles — hardware electrical engineer, engineering manager, senior engineering manager — leading iPhone development. TechCrunch described him as someone who “knows a thing or two about hardware, software and how people interact with them.”

That last part matters most. Apple’s institutional obsession isn’t really components — it’s the seam between hardware and the person holding it. Nine years inside that culture is nine years of treating “the user has to read a manual” as a design failure rather than a fact of life.

Yang has been fairly direct that this is the standard he brought with him. Speaking to TechCrunch, he put the goal in one sentence:

“If someone can use an iPhone, they’ll be able to use this.”

Then robotaxis

After Apple, Yang moved into autonomy. He was Director and Head of Advanced Hardware Engineering at Zoox from January 2019 to October 2020, then led AV hardware systems at Cruise, GM’s self-driving division.

This second act is the less obvious half of the Flow’s DNA, and arguably the more important one. Self-hitching and remote parking aren’t RV features that someone bolted on — they’re downstream of a decade of work on vehicles that perceive their surroundings and position themselves. Magic Hitch is a small, tightly-scoped autonomy problem solved by people who spent years on much harder versions of it.

The rest of the founding team follows the same pattern. CTO Stefan Solyom was a founding member of the Tesla Autopilot team, and before that spent seven years at Apple and ten at Volvo Cars working on safety and autonomous systems. Early staff came from Apple, Cruise, Lucid Motors and Tesla. Almost nobody came from the RV industry.

The trip that started it

In the summer of 2020, with the pandemic closing off most other kinds of travel, Yang took his family on an RV trip.

By his account, the family had a great time. He did not. TechCrunch reported he was “inspired by his own time in RVs, a product experience he hated.” The vacation worked; the product got in the way of it.

Anyone who has rented an RV recognises the specific texture of that frustration. Every subsystem has its own control panel and its own logic. Setting up camp is a checklist. Backing into a site requires a spotter and a certain tolerance for public marital conflict. None of it is hard exactly — it’s just tedious, and the tedium is concentrated at exactly the moments you were hoping to relax.

Yang founded Pebble Mobility in 2022. His framing of the opportunity has stayed consistent since:

“Consumers have been stuck with the same RV experience for decades. A lot of people want to get into such a lifestyle of freedom but are turned off by the pain points in today’s products.”

He also connected it to something broader — that remote and flexible work had changed who wants a vehicle like this. “We’ve seen a profound shift in how people live and work,” he told TechCrunch, pointing at the digital-nomad audience that traditional RV design never really addressed.

Where the design came from

The Flow’s look didn’t come from Yang alone. Pebble’s head of design is Page Beermann, previously of Faraday Future and BMW — and, as Fast Company noted, someone with an unusually direct qualification for the job: from ages two to five, Beermann lived in a Volkswagen van while his parents toured the world in it.

Beermann’s diagnosis of the category echoes Yang’s:

“Traditional RVs are pretty hard to use — they have a lot of disparate subsystems that all have their own control interfaces, and they’re quite challenging to operate. They haven’t really come into the modern era yet.”

Three influences show up clearly in the finished trailer:

Aerodynamics, treated as a first-order constraint. Pebble claims the Flow is about 300% more aerodynamic than a conventional travel trailer. That shape isn’t styling — it’s the reason nothing protrudes from the roof, no air conditioner hump, no wind dams. On an electric trailer towed by an electric truck, drag is the enemy that matters most.

Consumer electronics, not coachbuilding. An aluminium space frame, obsessive weight reduction, and a single on-board tablet controlling the whole vehicle rather than a wall of separate panels. Beermann described the leap as being “like taking a 20-year leap from one product to the next” in consumer-electronics terms.

The tiny-home movement. Fast Company noted the influence directly — dual-purpose everything, because 150 square feet doesn’t forgive single-use objects. The Murphy bed folds into a desk. The induction cooktop lifts out and works outside, a detail Beermann designed because, as a hobby cook, he was tired of being stuck indoors at the stove while everyone else sat by the fire.

And then the 270-degree wraparound glass, which is the one element that reads as pure design confidence. It’s the reason the interior feels like a room with a view rather than a corridor, and it’s why the optional smart privacy glass exists at all.

Money and momentum

Pebble emerged from stealth in mid-2023 with $13.6 million in seed and Series A funding from Lightspeed, Vision+ and UpHonest Capital, with about ten full-time employees at the time.

The Flow was revealed in October 2023, shown publicly at the LA Auto Show that November, and a production-intent version was unveiled at CES in January 2025 with pricing: $109,500 for the Standard, $135,500 with the Magic Pack, $175,000 for the Founders Edition.

Why the background matters

It would be easy to read “ex-Apple founder disrupts RVs” as a punchline. Plenty of consumer-electronics people have wandered into heavy manufacturing and discovered that welding is harder than firmware.

But the specific combination here is unusual and coherent. The iPhone years explain why a trailer would have one interface instead of nine. The Zoox and Cruise years explain why it can park itself. The bad 2020 camping trip explains why anyone thought those were the problems worth solving.

Whether that produces a durable company is a separate question, and an open one — long-term reliability, service coverage and resale value are all unproven for a young manufacturer. But the design brief was clearly written by someone who was annoyed in a very particular way, and you can feel that in the product.

Why this site exists

Our own version of Yang’s origin story is much less dramatic. A friend invited us to go camping in the Sierras for a weekend. I did some idle research, and a Pebble Flow ad turned up in my Reddit feed. I clicked through to their site and that was that — hooked.

My wife and I got as far as seriously discussing a purchase before buying a house instead, about a year ago. The Flow is still on the list. Until then, this site is where we keep track of it.
